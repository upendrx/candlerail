How to read this research

What a candle shows, what "R" means, why a candle pattern alone is not a strategy, how the studies decide where on the chart a pattern counts, and one real trade from start to finish.

10-minute readTap any ⓘ for a definitionEvery number is from the studies
The one idea to take away

A pattern is only a signal in the right place, in the right market.

The same hammer candle lost money when it was traded anywhere, and made money only at a support level in the direction of the trend. Every strategy in these studies is a checklist: pattern, place, trend, trigger, stop, target and size.

1What these studies do

A strategy is a set of exact rules: when to buy, where to give up, where to take profit, and how much to put in. A backtest runs those rules on years of real prices, as if they had been traded then, and pays the exchange's fees on every trade.

Because a computer does it, one study can test hundreds of versions of an idea on dozens of coins. The catch is that some versions will look good by pure luck, so every study keeps part of the data aside and only trusts rules that also worked there. Section 8 explains how.

2Reading a candle

Each candle is one period: one minute, 15 minutes, 4 hours, a day or a week, depending on the chart. It shows four prices.

3What "R" means

R is the amount you risk on one trade: what you lose if the stop is hit. Results are measured in R so that they mean the same thing on any account size.

  • −1R: the stop was hit and you lost what you risked.
  • +2R: the trade won twice what it risked.
  • +0.2R a trade on average: over 100 trades you gain 20 times your risk. If you risk $5 a trade, that is $100 over 100 trades.

You choose R yourself by choosing the stop and the position size. Change the numbers below to see how it works.

Fees are measured in R too

A market order on Binance futures costs about 0.06% each way with slippage, so about 0.12% a round trip. With a stop 0.1% away, that is 1.2R of fees on every trade: you lose more than your whole risk on fees alone. This is why the day-trading setups in Study 1 failed.

4Why a candle alone is not a strategy

A hammer says "sellers pushed price down and buyers pushed it back". That only means something where buyers had a reason to be: at a level where price turned before, and in a market that is rising.

Ignored

In the middle of nowhere

A hammer in a sideways drift, far from any level. Nothing to bounce from: it's noise. Traded like this, hammers lost money.

Traded

At support, with the trend

Price pulls back to where it turned up before, while the trend line rises beneath it. The hammer shows buyers defending that level.

Filtered out

At support, against the trend

The same hammer at the same kind of level, but the trend line is falling above price. Bounces in a falling market tend to fail; the trend filter skips it.

5How the studies find the right place

"At support" has to be exact for a computer to test it. These are the rules the studies used, in the order a trade has to pass them.

1. Find the swing levels

2. The pattern must form at that level

3. Only in the direction of the bigger trend

For day trades: longs only while price is above its 8-hour EMA, shorts only below it. For swing trades: above the 200-candle average. For the weekly strategy, the filter looks at bitcoin, not the coin itself: longs only while bitcoin's daily close is above its 50-day average, because most coins fall when bitcoin does.

4. A pattern on a bigger chart, a trigger on a smaller one

The strongest result used two timeframes. The weekly chart gives the context (a bullish engulfing week), and the daily chart gives the moment (the first day that closes green above the previous day's high). The trade waits for price to confirm the weekly signal instead of buying blindly at the week's open.

5. Then the exit and the size

Every trade has a stop fixed at entry (for example 2 ATR below, or just beyond the signal candle), a target or a time limit, and a size set so the stop loses 1R. Without these, a pattern is an opinion, not a strategy.

The checklist every strategy here follows

Pattern → at the right place → with the trend → confirmed by a trigger → stop, target and size decided before entry. A trade happens only when every item is true.

8How a study decides what works

Imagine picking a student by giving them last year's exam with the answers. They would score perfectly and learn nothing. Testing a trading rule on the same data it was chosen on is the same mistake.

Step 1

Choose on the first part

Every variant is tested on the first two-thirds of the data. Only those that pass a bar written down in advance are "selected".

Step 2

Check on the rest

Selected variants are then run on the last third, which played no part in choosing them. Those still profitable on most coins "survived".

Step 3

Check again elsewhere

The best survivors are run unchanged on coins or years they never saw. Study 1's survivor failed here; Study 2's and Study 4's held up.

What it can't do

Promise the future

A rule that worked on six years of unseen data can still stop working. It is evidence, not a guarantee.

9How to read the charts

Dot charts ("every variant, before and after")

Each dot is one version of a strategy. Left to right: how it did in the period it was chosen on. Bottom to top: how it did afterwards. Dots in the green corner made money in both. If the dots in that corner are few and scattered, the good results were luck.

Colour grids (heatmaps)

Rows are strategies, columns are timeframes. Green cells made money per trade, red lost. The number is the middle (median) result of all its versions.

Paired bars

Purple is the period the strategies were chosen on, green is afterwards. A strategy that is real keeps its green bar on the same side as its purple one.

Monthly bars and growth lines

Each bar is one month's gain or loss of the account. The shaded part is data the rule was never chosen on. The growth line shows what 1 dollar would have become, on a log scale.
